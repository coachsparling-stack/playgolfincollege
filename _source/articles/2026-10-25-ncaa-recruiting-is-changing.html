title: NCAA Recruiting Is Changing. Here's What Golf Parents Should Pay Attention To. date: 2026-10-25 issue: 6 description: Prize money rules, proposed official visit changes and an Ohio eligibility lawsuit: what each NCAA headline really means for a junior golf family. ---

It is easy to read an NCAA headline and assume the rule has changed for your child.

Sometimes it has. Sometimes a committee has introduced a proposal. Sometimes a court dispute affects particular athletes or schools.

Those distinctions matter. Here are three developments worth understanding.

1. Prize money rules before college have changed in Division I.

The NCAA's April 15 announcement permits prospects to accept prize money before enrollment without affecting eligibility under the updated Division I rule. The announcement does not change the prize money rules after enrollment.

For golfers, separate amateur status and tournament requirements still need attention. NCAA permission does not answer every eligibility question. [1]

2. Changes to official visits and coaching limits are proposals.

On October 7, the Division I Cabinet introduced a package that would allow schools to provide official visit transportation, accommodations and entertainment for a prospect and four accompanying individuals.

It would also remove limits on countable coaches while retaining limits on off campus recruiters.

The NCAA said adoption could occur as early as January 2027. These are proposed changes, not benefits families should assume are available today. [2]

3. Eligibility disputes deserve attention, especially in Ohio.

On October 16, the NCAA announced a federal lawsuit challenging the governor of Ohio's eligibility executive order.

The filing confirms a legal dispute. It does not, by itself, establish the outcome or guarantee an additional season for any particular player. [3]

For a recruiting family, the practical question is whether a coach's projected openings depend on a current player's unresolved eligibility.

What should parents do with this information?

When a headline seems relevant, ask:

  • Is this adopted, proposed or being challenged?
  • Which division and athletes does it cover?
  • When does it take effect?
  • Does it change a decision our family needs to make?

Then have the appropriate school compliance office confirm how it applies to your child.

You do not need to become an NCAA rules expert. You do need to recognize when a headline leaves out the detail that matters most to your family.

Be a lighthouse, not a tugboat.
