title: Hitting Balls Isn't Practice date: 2026-11-17 issue: 3 series: weekly description: How any practice should work, and why calling the shot before you look helps. Plus a new brain study on elite athletes and NCAA transfer data. ---

This week: what brain scans of elite athletes show about getting better, new NCAA research linking multiple transfers to lower graduation rates, and why the way most kids practice indoors this time of year is mostly wasted time.

One news item: The best players' brains work less, and notice more

A study published November 4 in the journal The Cerebellum scanned the brains of 41 elite baseball and softball players, from the Czech national team and the country's top league, each with at least 10 years of competition. Researchers from Prague, Paris and the University of Minnesota compared them with 46 adults who don't play sports while everyone tried to hit a moving target.

The experts hit it more often, 68% of the time against 53%. Their brains showed less activity doing it, especially in the areas we use for attention and effort. But when they missed by a little, their brains reacted more strongly than everyone else's.

Here's the basic science of skill behind that. Your brain learns by predicting what's about to happen, then checking what actually did. Early on, the prediction is fuzzy. The brain works hard, and small misses get lost in the noise. With years of good practice, the prediction gets sharp. The skill runs quietly, and a small miss stands out, so it gets fixed.

Keep in mind, this is one published study comparing two groups at one point in time. It shows a link, not that practice caused it, and it's baseball and softball, not golf.

How I read it: the best players aren't thinking harder. They're quieter, with fewer competing thoughts, and the small misses jump out at them. Success is in the details.

Source: Filip et al., "Motor Expertise Combines Lower Task Engagement with Enhanced Error Sensitivity," The Cerebellum, November 4, 2026.

Also in the news: Multiple transfers are associated with lower graduation rates

On November 11, the NCAA released research following nearly 36,000 Division I athletes who entered college in 2018–19. Within six years:

  • 88% of athletes who never transferred graduated.
  • 71% of athletes who transferred more than once graduated.

Keep in mind, this is research, not a rule change. These are overall Division I figures, not golf specific results. The study establishes an association; it does not prove transferring caused the difference. The cohort also entered college before today's recruiting environment.

Why it matters: "We can always transfer later" deserves more scrutiny as a college selection strategy. A better golf opportunity should be evaluated alongside progress toward a degree and the potential cost of additional semesters.

What I tell my families: add four questions to every transfer discussion.

  1. Which credits apply toward the intended degree, not merely transfer into the university?
  2. How many semesters remain until graduation?
  3. What will those semesters cost after confirmed financial aid?
  4. What specific roster opportunity is available, and how is the travel lineup selected?

This supports careful transfer planning, rather than discouraging a move that genuinely improves a player's situation.

Source: NCAA, "NCAA data shows lower graduation rates for multiple transfers," November 11, 2026, and the full report, Academic Impact of Multiple Transfers in Division I.

One idea: Hitting balls isn't practice

Walk into any indoor facility this time of year and you'll see it. A kid in a bay with a bucket, hitting driver after driver into a screen. A hundred balls in forty minutes. It feels like work, and everybody goes home thinking they practiced.

They didn't. They hit balls.

Here's how any practice should work, from the research on how people learn skills and from 25 years of watching what actually makes juniors better.

  • Have a plan. Know what you're working on before you walk in. Don't wing it, and don't just hit the shots you're already good at.
  • Work where shots are saved. Barzeski and Wedzik call it Separation Value in Lowest Score Wins: the parts of the game where getting better actually lowers your score.
  • Focus in short blocks. 20 to 25 minutes of real focus, then a few minutes to reflect: what went well, what you learned, what to improve. Then go back for another block.
  • About an hour and a half at a time. If you have more time, take a real break first, half an hour to an hour, then go again. How much you can do depends on how long you can stay focused and engaged.

Not every session has to be measured. There's a real place for technique work with your swing coach, slow exaggerated swings, and a lot of skill building. Winter is a great time for it.

But here's what very few juniors do regularly, and it's incredibly valuable: practice the way you play. Go through your full routine. See the shot. Commit to a specific shot to a specific target. Hit it and accept it.

Then add one step that makes it even better: call it before you look. Long or short, left or right, roughly how far. Then check. Remember this week's brain study: the best players notice the small misses. And in a 2001 study in the Journal of Motor Behavior, people who estimated their own miss before seeing the result learned the skill better than people who were just shown it. Indoors is perfect for this, because the launch monitor tells you exactly where it went. Most kids look first. Call it first, then look.

My players do this in Practice Lab, a private app I built for them, not for sale. But you don't need an app. Here's the simple version:

  • Pick one shot and one target. Write it down.
  • Full routine, see it, commit, hit it.
  • Call it before you look, then check.
  • In a notebook, write where it went and pass or fail on the routine, not the shot.
  • Do 50 balls like that and mark down how many of those had great routine and commitment.

For younger kids, keep it a game. One ball, one target, call it, check it, a little contest. No stats before about 12.

This week: make one session a measured one. And parents, ask one question: does practice have a plan and a score, or just a bucket of balls?
