Five to play five. Read this before you panic.

The NCAA changed a rule this week. Your feed is going to fill up with hot takes, and almost all of them will be about football. Let me give you the calm version and what it actually means for a junior golfer's family.

For a long time, college athletes had five years to play four seasons. There was almost always a wasted year built in. A redshirt. An injury year. A season on the bench. As of this week, Division I moved to five seasons over five years. That fifth season isn't something you have to earn through a waiver anymore. It's built in. The clock now starts the school year after a player turns 19 or first enrolls full time, whichever comes first.

That one change reshapes the math of college rosters. Here's what you need to see.

The rosters just got older.

When every player can use a full five seasons, fewer of them leave early. Coaches keep their experienced golfers longer. Turnover slows down. The lineup your child is trying to crack in two or three years is going to be older, deeper, and more tested than the one on campus today.

This isn't a doomsday headline. It's a fact to plan around. The freshman who walks on and plays in the fall is becoming a rare story. More often, a newcomer spends a year or two earning a spot in the lineup behind upperclassmen who aren't going anywhere.

Which means fewer spots open each year.

Simple math. If players stay five years and rosters turn over slower, fewer chairs open up each recruiting cycle. The top of Division I gets tighter. That pressure doesn't disappear. It pushes down and out, toward Division II, Division III, NAIA, and junior college, where a lot of golf careers are quietly thriving.

I want to be honest with you about one wrinkle, because you'll hear it debated. There's an open question about whether junior college years count against that five year clock. As the rule is written, they do. But that exact issue is being fought in court right now, with a trial set for early next year. So if anyone tells you the JUCO path is safe or free, they're guessing. The truth is, it's unsettled, and you shouldn't build a plan that depends on it until the courts rule.

So here's the move. Get realistic about fit.

This is the part I care about most, and it's where calm parents win.

When spots are scarce, the worst thing a family can do is chase a logo. Reaching for a program where your kid would be the eighth man on a seven man travel roster isn't ambition. It's a recipe for a transfer, a year of frustration, and a player who loses the love of the game sitting in a parking lot after a tournament they didn't get to play in.

The families who handle this well do something harder and smarter. They look honestly at where their child can actually play, contribute, and grow. A starting role at a strong Division II program will develop a golfer faster than four years on the bench at a big name Division I school. A great academic fit at a Division III where your kid competes every weekend beats a roster spot they can't crack.

Fit isn't the consolation prize. In this new world, fit is the strategy. Talent gets a kid noticed. Honest self assessment gets them a career.

This week

Sit down and ask one question together, out loud: where can my child actually play? Not where we wish. Not where it sounds impressive at a dinner party. Where they can be recruited, compete, contribute, and love it. Write down the programs that pass that test. That list is worth more than any ranking.
