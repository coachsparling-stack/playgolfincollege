This week: what happens to your heart rate under pressure, and why the questions you ask after a round matter more than you think.

One news item: What happens to your heart rate under pressure

A new study came out October 28. Researchers in Belgium put heart rate monitors on seven junior golfers, ages 13 to 16, all with handicaps under 12. The kids tried to make five putts in a row from about 10 feet.

After they made a putt, their heart rate went up about 5 beats a minute before the next one. After a miss, it went down. That makes sense. Make one and you've got a streak going, and every putt after that feels bigger. Miss and the pressure's off.

But the higher heart rate didn't make them miss. They made putts just as well either way. Hmm.

The pros feel it too. Golf Digest reported Rory McIlroy's heart rate was 135 on the 18th tee at the 2026 Masters. His normal resting heart rate is in the high 40s.

So what do great players do differently? A study at the University of Birmingham in England tested good golfers and beginners while they putted, sometimes with prize money on the line. Right before they hit the putt, the good players' heart rate slowed down about twice as much as the beginners'. Their brain activity calmed down too. And it didn't matter if money was on the line or not. They did it the same way every time.

I think that's a big part of being in the zone. Your heart can be racing walking up to the green. That's normal. Good players settle down right before they hit it, every time.

So here's what I want players to take from this. A fast heartbeat is not a problem. It just means you care. What matters is having a routine you do every single shot, whether you made the last one or missed it. That's how you settle down.

Take one slow breath before every shot, then do your routine.

When you feel your heart going, tell yourself, "Good. I'm ready." A Harvard study found people who said "I'm excited" before something stressful did better than people who tried to calm down.

Practice with something on the line. Make five in a row or play a game against a friend, so pressure feels normal.

For young kids, just make it a game and have fun with it.

This works off the course too. A big test, a job interview, a big meeting at work. Your heart's going to race. That's ok. Take a breath, do your routine and don't let the last thing that happened mess with the next one.

Keep in mind, these are small studies, and most of it was done in practice, not in tournaments.

Sources: Gielen et al., Frontiers in Psychology, October 28, 2026 (study); Cooke et al., Psychophysiology, 2014 (study); Golf Digest, April 26, 2026 (article); Brooks, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014 (summary)

One idea: What you ask is what you teach

What's the first thing you ask your kid after a round?

For most parents it's one of these. "What'd you shoot?" "How'd you finish?" "What happened on 12?" "How many putts?" "Did you beat so and so?" "Did you make the cut?"

Or this one. "You played great. If you'd just avoided those two 3 putts you'd be in the lead."

None of those are bad questions. But they're all about the score.

Your kids learn what you care about from the questions you ask. Not from what you tell them. From what you ask.

So if you ask "what'd you shoot" after every round, they figure the score is what matters to you. You don't mean it that way. You love them and you want to know how it went. But after a few years of that question, they think the score is what you care about most.

And "you played great, but..." doesn't work either. They don't hear "you played great." They hear the two 3 putts.

I can usually tell what parents ask at home by how a kid talks about their round. Some kids tell me their score and an excuse right away. "I shot 78, but the greens were terrible." Most of the time their parents ask about the score first. Other kids tell me about a good decision they made, or how they came back after a bad start. Their parents usually ask about that kind of stuff.

There's a lot of research on this. Kids who get praised for being smart start picking easy things so they look good, and they give up faster when it gets hard. Kids who get praised for their effort keep trying and get better. A Stanford study found kids can't tell what their parents think about talent, but they know exactly how their parents react when they fail. And in youth sports, kids whose parents focus on results have more anxiety. Kids whose parents focus on learning and getting better have less.

So why do we all do it? The score is easy. It's a number. It's harder to see a good decision or a good attitude after a double bogey. We also judge shots by how they turned out. If the 3 wood finds the fairway, it was a great call. If the same swing goes in the trees, it was a bad decision. And most of us don't realize what our questions are telling our kids.

Remember the heart rate study. Kids carry the last shot into the next one. They carry your reaction too.

So here's what to do. When they come off the course, say one thing. "I loved watching you play today." Or "I loved watching you compete." Or "I loved how you handled the tough stuff out there today." Then stop talking. Ask if they're hungry. Let them bring it up if they want to.

Wait 48 hours before you talk about the round. Then try questions like these.

  • What were you most proud of today?
  • What was your best decision out there?
  • How did you handle it when things got hard?
  • What was the most fun part?
  • What do you want to work on this week?

If your kid is young and just starting out, ask what they liked and who they played with. Don't ask about the score. If you've got a high school or college player, pick one question to ask first after every tournament this month and stick with it. And watch your face when they tell you their score. They're watching you.

This works at home too. "What'd you get on the test?" sends one message. "What did you learn today?" sends a different one.

You'll know it's working when they start telling you about their round without you asking. When they do, just listen.
